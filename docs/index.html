Research papers

The same skills on four landmark papers from economics, AI, search and mathematics. Each run ingested the paper itself (arXiv, the authors' or a university copy), outlined it section by section, and built one short around a small worked example, checked by a script and credited to the paper on screen.

Economics · Akerlof 1970

Why good used cars vanish

Buyers who can't see quality pay for the average car, so the best cars leave, the average falls, and only the lemon trades.

AI · Vaswani et al. 2017

Attention is a weighted average

Three words, real dot products and a softmax: “sat” attends mostly to “cat”, and its output is a weighted average of the values.

Search · Brin & Page 1998

PageRank: the random surfer

Four pages, one surfer who clicks forever. A page with a single link nearly ties the page with three, because that link comes from the busiest page.

Maths · Shannon 1948

Entropy counts questions

Guess a hidden letter with yes/no questions: the average number, 1.75, is the entropy, and the question tree is the shortest code.

Linear Algebra · MIT 18.06

Made from MIT OpenCourseWare 18.06SC with one request: “turn this course into complete notes and five shorts that show its arc.” Every short was storyboarded, coded, narrated, rendered, reviewed frame by frame and approved by agents following these skills.

Sessions 1.1–1.2

Two pictures of one system

Rows meet at a point; columns combine to reach b. Same answer, two views.

Sessions 1.7–1.11

What a matrix crushes

A rank-1 matrix keeps a line and flattens a plane: the four subspaces in 3D.

Sessions 2.1–2.3

Best fit: a right angle

The best-fit line's error is perpendicular to the columns, so AᵀAx̂ = Aᵀb.

Sessions 2.8–2.11

Follow the eigenvectors

Split a population along two eigenlines and A¹⁰⁰ becomes easy.

Session 3.5

Circle in, ellipse out

Every matrix sends some perpendicular pair to a perpendicular pair: the SVD.

Standalone

Why a positive test means ~9%

1000 dots, 10 sick: the false positives swamp the true ones. (A standalone short.)

Algorithms · MIT 6.006

The same skills on a different subject, from MIT OpenCourseWare 6.006: the agent crawled 166 sources, outlined all 21 lectures, and planned a three-short demo of the graph and dynamic-programming arc, each built on an example whose numbers a script recomputes by brute force.

Lecture 9

BFS grows level by level

The lecture's own exercise, solved level set by level set: each level is the unvisited neighbours of the last.

Lecture 13

Dijkstra trusts the smallest

Estimates fall as roads are relaxed; the smallest one left is always final. 6 minutes beats the direct 10.

Lecture 15

Recurse, but re-use

Fifteen calls collapse into six subproblems: the Fibonacci call tree folds into a DAG.

How it works

The agent does the teaching (choosing ideas, worked examples, proofs, storyboards, Manim code, and judging the result). Small scripts do everything mechanical and checkable.

course-to-shorts

The runbook: environment doctor, stage order, parallel subagents, and a publish gate that rejects stale work.

course-plan

Crawls the course (pages, PDFs, transcripts, navigation order), writes the outline and a shorts plan with verified examples.

course-notes

LaTeX template, real TeX builds, lint for placeholders and code-style maths, page previews, parallel per-unit builds.

course-video

shortkit for 9:16 Manim, real LaTeX in video, narration-timed beats, word-synced captions, live layout checks, contact sheets.

course-reels

Captions, part numbers and an ordered Instagram Reels queue; publishes through the Graph API only when you ask.
